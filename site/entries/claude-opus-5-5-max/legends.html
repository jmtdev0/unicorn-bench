A timeline in four ages

Legends through the ages

Four and a half thousand years of unicorns: carved on Bronze Age seals, argued over by philosophers, woven into tapestries, sold as medicine, and finally put to work on billion-dollar balance sheets.

  2. II The Middle Ages
  3. III The Age of the Horn

I

Antiquity

c. 2600 BCE – 500 CE · Seals, scrolls and a great deal of hearsay.

  1. c. 2600–1900 BCE

    The seal-carvers of the Indus

    In the cities of the Indus Valley Civilization, in today's Pakistan and northwest India, craftsmen carved thousands of small stone seals. The most common animal on them is a bull-like creature shown in profile with a single horn, usually standing before a mysterious object that may be an incense burner or a ritual stand.

    Nobody can yet read the Indus script on the seals, so we don't know what the creature was called. Archaeologists simply call it "the unicorn".

    See it: Indus seals are displayed in museums in New Delhi, Karachi and London.

  2. 481 BCE

    The last qilin of Lu

    Chinese tradition holds that a lin, the gentle, often single-horned beast later known as the qilin, was captured during a hunt in the state of Lu. Confucius took it as a sign that his age of virtue was ending, and the Spring and Autumn Annals stop at this event.

    A qilin, the story goes, had also appeared to his mother before he was born.

  3. c. 400 BCE

    Ctesias writes home

    The Greek physician Ctesias of Cnidus, serving at the Persian court, writes the first Western description of a unicorn: a wild ass of India, white-bodied and red-headed, with a horn that is white, black and crimson. Cups made from the horn, he says, protect against poison.

    He never went to India. He was repeating stories, which turned out to be the unicorn's main habitat for the next two thousand years.

    Meet the Indian wild ass in the Bestiary

  4. 4th century BCE

    Aristotle takes notes

    In his writings on animals, Aristotle mentions two one-horned creatures: the oryx and the "Indian ass". With the most respected naturalist of antiquity on record, the unicorn becomes a matter of natural history rather than fable.

  5. 3rd–2nd century BCE

    Lost in translation

    Scholars translating the Hebrew Bible into Greek meet the re'em, a mighty wild animal, and render it monokeros: "one horn". Latin and English translations follow suit, and the King James Bible of 1611 mentions unicorns nine times.

    The re'em was most likely the aurochs, a huge wild ox with two perfectly good horns.

  6. c. 77 CE

    Pliny's monster

    In his encyclopedic Natural History, Pliny the Elder describes the monoceros of India: a horse's body, a stag's head, an elephant's feet, a boar's tail and a single black horn two cubits long. It bellows deeply, he adds, and it cannot be captured alive.

  7. 2nd–4th century CE

    The maiden and the unicorn

    The Physiologus, an early Christian book of animal lore, tells how the unicorn, small and goat-like but too fierce for any hunter, can be caught only by a maiden: it lays its head in her lap and falls asleep. The tale will shape European unicorns for over a thousand years.

II

The Middle Ages

500–1500 · Purity, poison, and the occasional disappointing rhinoceros.

  1. 12th–13th century

    The age of bestiaries

    Illuminated bestiaries, lavish books of animal lore, are the bestsellers of medieval Europe, and nearly all of them include the unicorn. The favorite scene shows it resting its head in a maiden's lap while hunters close in with spears, a story read as an allegory of Christ.

    See it: the Aberdeen Bestiary (c. 1200), which the University of Aberdeen has digitized in full.

  2. 13th century

    Wonders of creation

    Persian and Arabic wonder-books, such as Zakariya al-Qazwini's cosmography, describe the ferocious karkadann, a one-horned giant able to skewer an elephant, and the shadhavar, whose hollow, branching horn plays music when the wind blows through it.

    Listen to the shadhavar in the Bestiary

  3. c. 1292

    Marco Polo's review

    Sailing home from China, Marco Polo spends months on Sumatra, where he finally meets unicorns: hairy beasts with elephant-like feet, a head like a wild boar's and a thick black horn, which love to wallow in mud.

    They are nothing like the European tales, he complains: “’Tis a passing ugly beast to look upon.” He had met a rhinoceros.

  4. 1414

    A giraffe for the emperor

    A giraffe, brought by sea from Bengal, arrives at the court of the Yongle Emperor. Courtiers hail it as a living qilin, a sign of heaven's favor, and a court painter records it with its keeper. To this day, kirin is the Japanese word for giraffe.

  5. 15th century

    Scotland chooses a unicorn

    Unicorns appear as supporters of the royal arms of Scotland, symbols of purity, pride and untameable strength. In the 1480s James III even strikes gold coins called "unicorns".

III

The Age of the Horn

1500–1800 · Tapestries, treasure chambers and a very profitable misunderstanding.

  1. c. 1495–1505

    The Hunt of the Unicorn

    Seven tapestries woven in the Southern Netherlands follow a unicorn hunt: the hunters gather, the unicorn purifies a poisoned stream with its horn, fights back, and is captured and killed. Yet the series ends, mysteriously, with the unicorn alive and resting inside a small round fence under a pomegranate tree.

    See it: The Met Cloisters, New York. Handwoven replicas hang in Stirling Castle, Scotland.

  2. c. 1500

    The Lady and the Unicorn

    Six tapestries woven in Flanders show a lady with a lion and a unicorn on a red field strewn with tiny flowers. Five represent the senses: touch, taste, smell, hearing and sight. The sixth, inscribed À mon seul désir ("to my only desire"), has puzzled historians ever since.

    See it: Musée de Cluny, Paris.

  3. 1577

    A sea unicorn for the queen

    On his second voyage to the Arctic, Martin Frobisher's crew finds a dead narwhal and takes its horn for Queen Elizabeth I. Spiders placed inside its hollow tip, the sailors report, promptly died: proof, they decided, that this was a genuine unicorn's horn.

  4. 1603

    The lion and the unicorn

    When James VI of Scotland also becomes James I of England, the royal coat of arms gains two supporters: the English lion and the Scottish unicorn. The unicorn wears a golden chain, because a free unicorn was thought too wild to trust.

    The old rivalry lives on in a nursery rhyme: "The lion and the unicorn were fighting for the crown."

    See it: unicorns crown many Scottish market crosses, including Edinburgh's Mercat Cross.

  5. c. 1612

    A unicorn among the stars

    The cartographer Petrus Plancius places a new constellation on a celestial globe: Monoceros, the Unicorn, in a dim patch of sky beside Orion.

    Find the Unicorn in tonight's sky

  6. 1638

    The hoax unravels

    The Danish physician and collector Ole Worm shows his audience that the "unicorn horns" treasured across Europe are actually the tusks of narwhals. Prices begin a long, slow slide.

  7. 1660s

    A throne of tusks

    Denmark's absolute monarchs commission a coronation throne built largely of narwhal tusk, still "unicorn horn" in spirit, guarded by three life-size silver lions. It was used to anoint the kings of Denmark for nearly two centuries.

    See it: Rosenborg Castle, Copenhagen.

  8. 1758

    Science makes it official

    Carl Linnaeus names the greater one-horned rhinoceros Rhinoceros unicornis, and the narwhal gets its own official name too: Monodon monoceros, "one tooth, one horn".

A feature

The great horn hoax

For centuries, the most expensive medicine in Europe was a whale's tooth.

From the Middle Ages onward, "unicorn horn", or alicorn, was believed to neutralize poison. Kings and nobles drank from alicorn cups, touched horns to their food, and kept whole tusks in their treasuries; apothecaries sold it ground into powder. The horns were in fact narwhal tusks, traded down from the Arctic by Norse and later merchants, and they could fetch more than their weight in gold.

With fortunes at stake, buyers wanted proof. Recorded tests for a genuine horn included:

  1. The spider test. Draw a circle with the horn and put a spider inside. A true horn, it was said, would keep the spider from escaping.
  2. The pigeon test. Give poison to two pigeons, and powdered horn to one of them. If that one survives, the horn is real.
  3. The water test. Place the horn in water: a genuine one was said to make it bubble.

Please do not test any of this at home, on spiders, pigeons, or anyone else.

IV

Modern times

1800 – today · From nursery books to startup decks to fossil labs.

  1. 1871

    A bargain with Alice

    In Lewis Carroll's Through the Looking-Glass, the Lion and the Unicorn fight for the crown. Meeting Alice, the Unicorn decides she must be a "fabulous monster", and proposes a deal: "if you'll believe in me, I'll believe in you."

  2. 1968

    The Last Unicorn

    Peter S. Beagle publishes The Last Unicorn, about a unicorn who sets out to discover what happened to the rest of her kind. The 1982 animated film makes it a classic for a generation.

  3. 2013

    A new kind of unicorn

    Venture capitalist Aileen Lee coins "unicorn" for privately held startups valued at over a billion dollars. When she counted, there were 39 of them. They have since become considerably less rare than the original.

  4. 2015

    Unicode unicorns

    The unicorn face is approved as part of Unicode 8.0, and a pastel, rainbow-maned herd takes over phones, cafés and birthday cakes worldwide.

  5. 2018

    The Siberian unicorn, redated

    Radiocarbon dating shows that Elasmotherium, the giant rhinoceros nicknamed the Siberian unicorn, survived until at least 39,000 years ago, long enough to share the steppe with modern humans.
